# Speed-to-Power V3: Transmission Development Readiness in Northern Virginia

## Working Paper Draft

**September 21, 2026**

## Abstract

Northern Virginia's data-center market has created a transmission-planning problem at a scale that cannot be represented by average electricity prices or regional reserve margins alone. This paper develops a third layer of the Speed-to-Power framework by moving from regional markets and utility tariffs to documented transmission projects, delivery-point queue rules, and load-serving substations in Dominion Energy Virginia's Northern Virginia system.

The analysis combines Dominion Energy and Virginia State Corporation Commission records for the large-load connection queue, the Twin Creeks-to-Apollo 230 kV project, the eastern Loudoun 500 kV reliability loop, and later transmission reinforcements. The public record shows approximately 70 GW of large-load delivery-point requests advancing through Dominion's queue in early 2026, compared with a cited Dominion Zone all-time peak of 24,678 MW. The formal process applies to data-center requests of approximately 100 MW or greater and caps individual delivery-point requests at 300 MW.

A 2024 transmission application provides unusually detailed evidence for five proposed load-serving substations: Twin Creeks, Sycolin Creek, Starlight, Lunar, and Apollo. Their requested ten-year loads total 1,372 MW. The filing also documents whether limited bridging power was available before permanent infrastructure.

The paper introduces a Transmission Development Readiness Index (TDRI) based on development stage, regulatory maturity, schedule specificity, and explicit load linkage. The index is deliberately not a capacity estimate. Its purpose is to distinguish mature, sourceable transmission development from early-stage or uncertain infrastructure without claiming knowledge of unused substation headroom.

## 1. Research question

How do transmission-project maturity, utility queue rules, site-readiness requirements, documented load commitments, and infrastructure sequencing shape the plausible path to power for 100–500 MW data-center loads in Northern Virginia?

## 2. Why the unit of analysis changes in V3

V1 compared regional markets.

V2 compared utility commercial structures.

Neither layer identifies whether a specific development area has a mature transmission pathway.

V3 therefore shifts the unit of analysis to:

- transmission reinforcement projects;
- proposed load-serving substations;
- delivery-point requests;
- public queue rules; and
- documented infrastructure schedules.

This still stops short of claiming site-level capacity because public records generally do not disclose the full power-flow, contingency, protection, transformer, or substation-headroom information required for such a conclusion.

## 3. Dominion's large-load queue

Dominion's February 2026 filing reports approximately 25 GW of large-load requests with projected connection dates through the end of 2031 and approximately 45 GW of additional requests assigned to study batches.

Approximately 70 GW were therefore advancing through the queue.

The filing compared this with the Dominion Zone all-time peak of 24,678 MW recorded on January 23, 2025.

The requested load in the queue is not equivalent to a demand forecast. Some projects may be delayed, resized, relocated, or not built.

The figure nevertheless demonstrates the planning scale confronting the transmission system.

## 4. Queue mechanics

The proposed standards apply to delivery-point requests associated with data-center loads of approximately 100 MW or more.

Each delivery-point request is capped at 300 MW.

Requests are studied in batches of approximately ten delivery-point requests, with each batch typically representing 2–3 GW of aggregate load.

The four project stages are:

1. Project Initiation
2. Project Feasibility
3. Project Development
4. Project Execution

### 4.1 Initiation

The customer submits load characteristics, voltage and timing requirements, site information, and preliminary engineering materials.

Dominion performs an initial viability assessment that considers:

- sufficient land;
- constructible interconnection routes; and
- environmental conditions.

### 4.2 Feasibility

To progress, the customer must provide a zoning conformance letter and a 30% engineering site plan.

Dominion evaluates constructible electrical solutions and produces a feasibility report.

### 4.3 Development

The customer must progress permits and provide a 100% grading plan and construction one-line diagram.

Dominion performs detailed engineering, prepares construction bid packages, and obtains required regulatory approvals.

### 4.4 Execution

The final stage covers detailed design, construction, energization, and transfer to operations and maintenance.

This staged structure makes clear that speed to power is partly determined by customer site readiness, not just utility engineering.

## 5. Implication of the 300 MW delivery-point cap

A 500 MW campus cannot enter the public framework as one 500 MW delivery-point request.

At minimum:

| Campus load | Minimum delivery-point requests |
|---:|---:|
| 100 MW | 1 |
| 300 MW | 1 |
| 500 MW | 2 |

The filing also states that campus-style developments requiring multiple substations on contiguous land are aligned and subject to staged advancement tied to demonstrated load ramp-up thresholds.

This creates a direct analytical link between phased computing deployment and transmission planning.

## 6. Twin Creeks-to-Apollo load evidence

The 2024 application describes approximately 1,372 MW of projected customer and emerging load served by five substations.

| Substation | Requested ten-year load | Target in-service in filing | Bridging power |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Twin Creeks | 300 MW | June 2026 | Pleasant View, 30 MVA |
| Sycolin Creek | 300 MW | September 2026 | None |
| Starlight | 255 MW | June 2028 | None |
| Lunar | 278 MW | February 2028 | Edwards Ferry, 30 MVA |
| Apollo | 239 MW | March 2028 | Edwards Ferry, 30 MVA |

The source distinguishes permanent load-serving infrastructure from temporary bridging capability.

That distinction matters because temporary power may support early commissioning without representing the final campus requirement.

V3 preserves the filing-era dates as evidence but does not assume a facility is operational merely because its target date has passed.

## 7. Eastern Loudoun 500 kV loop

### 7.1 Wishing Star-Mars

Dominion reports that the 500 kV GIS was energized in January 2026, temporary overhead bypass was energized in February 2026, and construction was complete in May 2026.

This is the most mature large reinforcement in the V3 project set.

### 7.2 Aspen-Golden

The SCC issued a final order in February 2025.

Dominion's current timeline lists construction beginning in January 2027 and the SCC-approved in-service date as June 2028.

### 7.3 Golden-Mars

The SCC application was filed March 28, 2025.

The SCC issued a final order on June 29, 2026 approving Route 3A.

The project completes another segment of the eastern Loudoun 500/230 kV reinforcement system.

## 8. Later transmission projects

### BECO-Firehouse

This project would add two new 230 kV lines connecting a new Firehouse Substation to BECO.

Dominion filed the SCC application May 8, 2026.

The public project page expects the new lines to be in service by early 2029.

### Global Plaza Extension

This proposed double-circuit 230 kV project would connect two existing transmission lines without requiring a new substation.

Dominion plans an SCC filing in December 2026, construction from mid-2028 through late 2029, and in-service status by the end of 2029.

### Morrisville-Wishing Star

The 500/230 kV project adds a new bulk-transfer path toward Northern Virginia and includes expansion of Morrisville Substation.

Dominion filed its SCC application in February 2026.

### Commonwealth Energy Express

This proposed HVDC project would connect Heritage Substation in Brunswick County with Mosby Substation in Loudoun County across approximately 200 miles.

V3 classifies the project as early development because the current public source set does not provide a final in-service date.

## 9. Transmission Development Readiness Index

V3 introduces a simple index to organize the public evidence.

[
TDRI =
0.40D +
0.30R +
0.15S +
0.15L
]

Where:

- (D) = development-stage score
- (R) = regulatory-maturity score
- (S) = schedule-specificity score
- (L) = explicit-load-linkage score

The weights are intentionally simple and transparent.

### 9.1 What the index measures

The TDRI answers:

> How mature and sourceable is the documented transmission-development pathway?

### 9.2 What it does not measure

The TDRI does not estimate:

- unused MW;
- thermal capacity;
- voltage stability;
- short-circuit duty;
- transformer headroom;
- N-1 deliverability;
- interconnection upgrade cost;
- or guaranteed time to power.

Those require engineering data that are not generally available in the public project record.

## 11. Key finding

The strongest result from V3 is that Northern Virginia's path-to-power problem has become a **sequencing problem**.

The public record shows three interacting sequences:

1. customer site-readiness milestones;
2. load-serving 230 kV substation development; and
3. regional 500 kV / bulk-transfer reinforcement.

A campus cannot be understood by looking at only one of these layers.

## 12. Next stage

V4 should create candidate development-area records and combine:

- specific service territory;
- nearest public transmission assets;
- known project dependencies;
- zoning status;
- route constructibility;
- service-voltage assumptions;
- target load ramp;
- utility queue requirements;
- planned transmission completion;
- and cost-responsibility rules.

The model should continue to avoid unsupported estimates of spare capacity.

## 13. Conclusion

Northern Virginia provides unusually detailed public evidence on how hyperscale load becomes transmission infrastructure.

The record shows that the path to power is governed not just by megawatts, but by customer readiness, delivery-point sequencing, multi-substation campus planning, regulatory approval, and the completion of both local 230 kV and regional 500 kV reinforcements.

Speed-to-Power V3 converts those records into a reproducible transmission-development framework while preserving the boundary between public evidence and non-public engineering capacity.
